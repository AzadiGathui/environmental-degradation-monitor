# Land Use and Land Cover Change in and Around Kenyan Protected Areas: Research Support for a U-Net Capstone Project

## TL;DR
- **Kenya's protected areas face documented, accelerating encroachment** — dramatized by the June 8, 2026 arrest of former Chief Justice David Maraga while protesting construction inside Nairobi National Park, and quantified by a 68% average decline in Kenyan wildlife from 1977–2016 (Ogutu et al., PLOS ONE, 2016) — making automated satellite monitoring both urgent and defensible.
- **U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) is a standard, heavily-cited (≈88,770 citations per Semantic Scholar) encoder–decoder architecture** whose augmentation-first design, low compute footprint, and strong performance on small/imbalanced datasets make it a well-justified choice for LULC segmentation under GPU and time constraints.
- **The main technical risk is domain shift** from training on an Indian dataset and applying it to Kenya; this is a recognized problem in remote sensing with an established domain-adaptation literature that the report should explicitly acknowledge and propose to mitigate (fine-tuning on Kenyan tiles).

---

## Key Findings

- **Recent flashpoints (Section 1):** The single most attention-grabbing recent event is the June 8, 2026 arrest of former Chief Justice David Maraga during a protest against the excision of ~75 acres of Nairobi National Park for a 1,300-car parking lot and relocated animal orphanage. In the Maasai Mara, a Ritz-Carlton luxury tented camp opened August 15, 2025 on the Sand River migration corridor, triggering a lawsuit dismissed on procedural grounds in April 2026. Mau Forest evictions have removed more than 50,000 people since 2009. Karura Forest is the symbolic birthplace of Kenyan forest activism through Wangari Maathai's 1998–99 campaign.
- **Statistics (Section 2):** Kenya lost roughly 400,390 ha of tree cover 2001–2024 (~12.1% of 2000 cover); the Mau Forest Complex has lost about a quarter of its area since 2000. Wildlife declined 68% nationally (1977–2016).
- **Mitigation (Section 3):** Electric fencing (Aberdare/Mt Kenya, Rhino Ark) cut human-wildlife conflict by up to 97%; community conservancies contribute 11% of Kenya's conservation land; but KWS faces a ~KSh 12 billion annual funding gap limiting enforcement.
- **History/global (Section 4):** Encroachment is rooted in colonial land alienation (~7.2 million acres of "White Highlands"). Globally, 32.8% of protected land is under intense human pressure (Jones et al., Science, 2018).
- **Remote sensing value (Section 5):** Multiple peer-reviewed Kenyan studies show satellite-based LULC classification detecting forest loss (e.g., Mau Complex forest cover down ~46%; Eastern Mau 49% of natural vegetation cleared by 2003).
- **Technical validation (Section 6):** U-Net is standard for remote sensing LULC; augmentation (rotation/flips) and class weighting are established practices; India→Kenya domain adaptation is the key caveat to address.

## Details

### SECTION 1 — RECENT NEWS ON ENCROACHMENT INTO SPECIFIC KENYAN PROTECTED AREAS

#### (a) Nairobi National Park — the David Maraga incident
On **Monday, June 8, 2026**, former Chief Justice **David Maraga** was arrested during a protest against planned construction inside Nairobi National Park. Police fired tear gas at protesters marching outside the park carrying banners denouncing land grabs. Maraga was detained at Lang'ata Police Station and, after release, staged a sit-in refusing to leave until fellow detainees were freed: *"I will not leave this station until all those detained are released"* (The Standard, June 8, 2026). He wrote on X: *"Our national heritage and environment must be safeguarded from greed and unnecessary destruction without public participation"* (ABC News/AP; Mongabay, June 8, 2026).

The protest targeted the planned excision of a **75-acre parcel** of the park to create a parking space, plus KWS plans to relocate the Nairobi Animal Orphanage near Bomas of Kenya. Maraga said he was arrested during a *"procession to present a petition to the Kenya Wildlife Services against the excision of part of the Nairobi National Park to construct a 1300-car parking lot"* (Tuko, June 8, 2026). KWS Director-General **Prof. Erustus Kanga** defended the project: *"The New Nairobi Animal Orphanage is not a privatisation of Nairobi National Park... It is not the alienation of protected land. It is not a private commercial or real estate development."* KWS said the facility will occupy 89 acres — about 0.31% of the park — costing between KSh 3 billion and KSh 4 billion (Daily Nation, June 8, 2026). **Amnesty International Kenya** expressed solidarity: *"Nairobi National Park is not for sale; our public spaces, our environment, and our rights cannot be traded away behind closed doors."*

**Earlier SGR controversy (context).** The Standard Gauge Railway route through Nairobi National Park was the major earlier flashpoint. In September 2016, hundreds of demonstrators led by the **Kenya Coalition for Wildlife Conservation and Management** staged the **#OccupyLang'ataRoad** protest; activists including **Kahindi Lekalhaile** demanded the resignation of KWS chairman **Dr. Richard Leakey** (Citizen Digital; Capital FM, Sept 16, 2016). A committee approved a route that would have compromised about 20% of the park, later shelved after the Environment Ministry protested; an EIA showed the realigned SGR would still destroy about **46.7 ha** of park habitat (The Star; SWARA Magazine). In March 2018, Transport CS James Macharia dismissed conservationists as *"busybodies."* UNEP's *Perspective No. 32* ("Will the Iconic Park Survive?") stated the park *"is experiencing the greatest encroachment since its inception in 1946."*

**Nairobi National Park facts.** Established/gazetted in **1946**, it was the **first national park in Kenya**, a project of conservationist Mervyn Cowie; Maasai pastoralists were removed when it was created (Britannica; New World Encyclopedia; KWS). It covers **117 km² (≈28,963 acres)**, fenced on three sides but open on the south to the Kitengela/Athi-Kaputiei plains. The park is only the protected core of the wider **~2,000 km² Athi-Kaputiei ecosystem** (Friends of Nairobi National Park, FoNNAP). The migratory wildebeest population using the dispersal area **collapsed from over 30,000 in the 1970s to about 509 by 2014** — a 99–100% local decline in key sectors — due to fences, poaching, and expansion of settlements, roads and Kitengela town (Said, Ogutu et al., *Journal for Nature Conservation*, 2016; ILRI, Nov 18, 2016). ILRI notes wildlife in the plains south of the park fell by **over 70% between 1977 and 2002**. **Key organization: Friends of Nairobi National Park (FoNNAP).**

#### (b) Mau Forest
The Mau Forest Complex (~400,000+ ha, the largest figure cited is ~455,000 ha across 22 blocks) is Kenya's largest indigenous montane forest and largest water tower, feeding 12 rivers and providing about **60% of Lake Victoria's inflow**. The Kenya Water Tower Agency says **a quarter of the forest has been lost since in-migration surged around 2000 — some 100,000 ha (247,000 acres)** — with the Maasai Mau section most affected (Mongabay, Jan 2022). Officers from KWS, KFS and national police have **evicted more than 50,000 people from more than 45,000 ha since 2009**. Human Rights Watch found the 2019 evictions used excessive force: houses burned, people beaten, crops destroyed, with **nine deaths reported**. Evictions are politically charged and entangled with the **Ogiek indigenous community** (~40,000 people), who won landmark **African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights rulings (2017 and 2022)** recognizing their ancestral ownership; the government nonetheless resumed evictions in November 2023 (Minority Rights Group; ReliefWeb, Nov 2023). Historical driver: Mau was cleared for tea plantations from the 1920s, with excisions to politically connected beneficiaries such as Kiptagich Tea Estate (Forest Trends). **Key figures/organizations: Kenya Water Tower Agency, Mau Conservation Trust (Jackson Kamoe), Ogiek Peoples' Development Program, Narok County Government.**

#### (c) Karura Forest
Karura (1,063 ha, Nairobi) is the symbolic heart of Kenyan forest defense. In **1998–99**, Nobel Peace laureate **Wangari Maathai** and the **Green Belt Movement** fought government plans to allocate the forest to private developers for housing and a golf course. On a January 1999 morning, Maathai and supporters were **violently attacked by hired guards** while attempting to plant trees; footage of the bloodshed outraged Kenya and the world and helped delegitimize the Moi government (Mongabay; Global Nonviolent Action Database). President Moi **banned the allocation of public land in August 1999**. More recent threats included the 2009 allocation of part of the forest to NEMA and 2016 concerns over commercial development of the Sigiria block, both opposed by the Green Belt Movement. Karura became a symbol against land-grabbing in Kenya. **Key figures/organizations: Wangari Maathai (Green Belt Movement), Friends of Karura Forest (FKF), Karanja Njoroge.**

#### (d) Maasai Mara — the Ritz-Carlton controversy
The **Ritz-Carlton Masai Mara Safari Camp** (20 suites, nightly rates from **$3,500** — around 30% more than the average Kenyan's annual income per World Bank) opened **August 15, 2025** on a bend in the Sand River during the Great Migration. Maasai elder **Meitamei Olol Dapash**, director of the **Institute for Maasai Education, Research and Conservation (MERC)**, filed a lawsuit **August 12, 2025** against Ritz-Carlton, parent **Marriott International**, local developer **Lazizi Mara Limited** and Kenyan authorities, alleging the camp obstructs a crucial wildebeest migration corridor with no valid Environmental Impact Assessment (NBC News; Reuters; The Kenya Times). The **Environment and Land Court at Narok dismissed the petition in April 2026 on procedural grounds** (not on the merits), ruling it should first pass through the National Environment Tribunal (Mongabay, April 2026; Greenpeace Africa). Authorities cited GPS collar data from **1999–2002** to argue no migration impact; critics called 23-year-old data inadequate. Narok County acknowledged over-tourism had harmed the Mara's environment. **Key figures/organizations: Meitamei Olol Dapash (MERC), Osotua Green Alliance (Yiamat), Greenpeace Africa, Narok County Government.**

### SECTION 2 — GENERAL ENCROACHMENT STATISTICS AND PATTERNS

- **Deforestation rate:** Kenya's national Forest Reference Level report estimates deforestation at **103,368 ha/year (0.17% of national area)**, partly offset by ~90,477 ha/year reforestation (KFS/UNFCCC, 2020). Forest cover is ~6% of land area (6.2% in 2002 to 5.9% in 2018).
- **Tree cover loss:** **~400,390 ha (~12.1% of 2000 cover)** lost 2001–2024 (Global Forest Watch/Hansen dataset).
- **Historic loss:** Kenya lost approximately **1.2 million ha of forest (≈25% of cover) between 1990 and 2000** (RCMRD/FAO figures).
- **Wildlife decline:** Ogutu, Piepho, Said et al. (*PLOS ONE* 11(9), 2016; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0163249) found, using aerial surveys covering 88% of Kenya's land surface, that **"wildlife numbers declined on average by 68% between 1977 and 2016,"** with the most extreme declines (72–88%) for warthog, lesser kudu, Thomson's gazelle, eland, oryx, topi, hartebeest, impala, Grevy's zebra and waterbuck. In the Mara specifically, wildebeest fell ~81% (from over 123,000 in 1977 to under 20,000 in 2016) and Serengeti-Mara migrants fell 73% (588,000 in 1979 to 157,000 in 2016).
- **Drivers:** Shifting cultivation reportedly responsible for 85% of forest loss; commercial agriculture and mining ~4% (Forest Trends). Cattle numbers nearly doubled 2000–2020 to 21.6 million.
- **Hotspots:** Mau Forest Complex (esp. Maasai Mau, Eastern Mau), the Mara/Kitengela dispersal plains, coastal forests, and Aberdare/Mt Kenya forest edges.
- **PADDD:** 91% of Kenya's protected areas shrank over 100 years; downsizing is the predominant disturbance (Mongabay/WWF PADDDtracker, 2014).
- **Deforestation alerts:** 134,201 alerts covering 1.65 kha, 99% high-confidence single-system (GFW-derived).
- **Agencies:** **Kenya Forest Service (KFS)** manages gazetted forests; **Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS)** manages parks/wildlife; **Narok County** manages the Maasai Mara Reserve and Maasai Mau; **Kenya Water Tower Agency (KWTA)** coordinates water-tower rehabilitation.

### SECTION 3 — MITIGATION MEASURES AND EFFECTIVENESS

- **Electric fencing.** The **Aberdare fence** (400 km, completed 2009, ~KSh 800 million; Rhino Ark + KWS/KFS) is among Africa's largest conservation fences; crop raids fell **over 90%**. The **Mt Kenya fence** (60 km assessed) reduced human-wildlife conflict by **97% — from an average 117 incidents/year (2004–2014) to about 3/year (2016–2018)** per KWS records (Rhino Ark, citing the UTaNRMP 2019 assessment). *Caveat:* peer-reviewed work notes elephants still breach fences in some Aberdare sections, prompting community retaliation.
- **Community conservancies.** Per KWCA (2021), cited in *Frontiers in Conservation Science* (2024): **"over 20% of Kenya's land mass is under a conservation or protection framework: national parks and reserves (8%); forests (2%); and wildlife conservancies (11%)."** The **Northern Rangelands Trust (NRT, founded 2004)** supports peace, security, and carbon projects across northern Kenya. *Caveat:* in 2025 a court ruled two NRT conservancies illegally established, USAID funding ended, and its carbon project was suspended (Mongabay, May 2025).
- **Evictions.** Used repeatedly in Mau; environmentally motivated but human-rights-problematic and politically fraught; encroachers tear down fences and return.
- **Ranger patrols / technology.** KWS uses drones, GPS tracking, and night-vision; it passed out 1,274 new rangers in 2024. Kenya has been highlighted by the UN as a model in combating elephant and rhino poaching.
- **Funding constraint (the central motivation for cheap satellite monitoring).** KWS faces a **~KSh 12 billion annual gap**: FY2024/25 generated **KSh 7.92 billion against a KSh 19.79 billion requirement**; ~72% of budget goes to personnel; the top 5 parks generate 73% of revenue; ~90% of internal revenue is tourism-dependent. KWS proposed its first fee review in 18 years in 2025.

### SECTION 4 — HISTORICAL AND GLOBAL CONTEXT

- **Colonial land alienation.** From the **1902 Crown Lands Ordinance**, the British alienated **~7.2 million acres** of fertile central highlands as the **"White Highlands,"** reserved for Europeans **1902–1961**, displacing Kikuyu and other communities to reserves and fueling the Mau Mau uprising (Wikipedia; "Historical Land Injustices in Kenya," 2022). Post-independence, land was not returned to original owners but passed to the political class, entrenching a land-grabbing culture that recurs in the Mau, Karura and Nairobi Park cases.
- **Global protected-area pressure.** Jones, Venter, Fuller, Allan, Maxwell, Negret & Watson (*Science* 360(6390):788–791, 18 May 2018; DOI 10.1126/science.aap9565): **"we show that 6 million square kilometers (32.8%) of protected land is under intense human pressure. For protected areas designated before the Convention on Biological Diversity was ratified in 1992, 55% have since experienced human pressure increases."** The most impacted PAs are in Asia, Europe, and Africa.
- **Challenges for rangers/experts:** funding gaps, population pressure, political interference, weak/patchy monitoring, and climate change exacerbating human-wildlife conflict.

### SECTION 5 — HOW REMOTE SENSING / LULC MODELS SUPPORT CONSERVATION

- **Documented Kenyan cases (directly supports the problem statement):**
  - **Ayuyo & Sweta (University of Nairobi):** Landsat classification (1973–2010) of the Mau Complex found **farmlands increased over 160% while forest cover decreased about 46%**, with Maasai Mau the most affected.
  - **Were et al. (2013, *Applied Geography*; DOI 10.1016/j.apgeog.2013.03.017):** multi-temporal Landsat of Eastern Mau/Lake Nakuru basin found **49% of natural vegetation cleared by 2003**, attributed partly to illegal encroachment and the 2001 excision of ~353 km².
  - **Baldyga et al. (2007, *African Journal of Ecology*):** about **one-fifth of upper Njoro catchment forest lost 1986–2003**; classification accuracy 86–90% (kappa 0.81–0.88).
  - **Land (MDPI, 2024, 13(9):1443):** agricultural expansion is the chief driver of LULC change in the Mara/Mau basin, particularly since 2015.
- **Global value:** Remote sensing provides comprehensive, high-resolution, regularly sampled land-cover data; ML methods enable scalable protected-area habitat monitoring (Plas et al., 2025, *Ecological Solutions and Evidence*, DOI 10.1002/2688-8319.70040). Commercial services (e.g., UP42) offer near-real-time encroachment detection.
- **Benefits for conservation:** early detection of encroachment, quantifying habitat loss, monitoring at scale, informing policy, and cost-effectiveness versus ground patrols — a decisive argument given the KWS funding gap. Random Forest and deep-learning classifiers have been used to map forest-encroachment drivers with >81% accuracy (e.g., Nepal study, *Spatial Information Research*, 2025) and to characterize encroachment typologies in national parks (Kerinci Seblat, Indonesia).

### SECTION 6 — TECHNICAL VALIDATION

**U-Net as standard.** U-Net was introduced by **Olaf Ronneberger, Philipp Fischer, and Thomas Brox** in *"U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation"* (MICCAI 2015; arXiv:1505.04597; DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-24574-4_28). It is an encoder–decoder ("U-shaped") architecture: a contracting path captures context and a symmetric expanding path with **skip connections** enables precise localization; it was explicitly designed to *"train end-to-end from very few images"* using **heavy data augmentation**. It is **one of the most-cited papers in the field (≈88,770 citations per Semantic Scholar, 2026)**. U-Net is now standard in remote sensing LULC — *"one widely employed neural network for remote sensing image land cover classification is the U-Net network, which is a classical semantic segmentation network"* (*Scientific Reports*, 2023) — and is described as *"nowadays among the most popular deep learning algorithms for LULC mapping."*

**Architecture comparison (defensible choice under constraints).** In a benchmark on the Inria aerial dataset comparing FCN-8s, U-Net, SegNet, PSPNet and DeepLab v2, **U-Net and DeepLab performed best**; FCN was the largest model while SegNet and U-Net had the smallest FLOPs due to their encoder–decoder structure; U-Net and DeepLab excelled at edge prediction (arXiv:1905.10231). U-Net has been shown to outperform DeepLabV3+ and SegNet on some datasets while using **fewer parameters and less processing time**. Crucially, a directly relevant Indian study — *"Exploring U-Net, FCN, SegNet, PSPNet, Mask R-CNN and using DeepLabV3+ for Multiclass Semantic Segmentation on Satellite images of Western Ghats"* — used **Indian Remote Sensing (IRS) LISS-III** multispectral imagery (4 bands: Blue, Green, Red, NIR; 13,500 labeled images) for LULC, reinforcing that U-Net is a well-established choice on an Indian training dataset.

| Architecture | Key mechanism | Tradeoff vs. U-Net |
|---|---|---|
| **U-Net (2015)** | Encoder–decoder + skip connections | Baseline: low FLOPs, excellent on small/imbalanced data, sharp edges |
| **FCN (2014)** | Fully-convolutional, coarse upsampling | Largest params, coarser edges |
| **SegNet (2015)** | Pooling-index upsampling | Smallest params but weaker boundary detail |
| **PSPNet (2017)** | Pyramid pooling for multi-scale context | Higher compute; strong on large scenes |
| **DeepLabv3+ (2018)** | Atrous convolution + ASPP + decoder | Comparable/better accuracy but heavier; good multi-scale |
| **SegFormer / transformers** | Self-attention | Can exceed CNNs given large data, but data- and GPU-hungry |

Under **GPU and time constraints with a limited/imbalanced dataset, U-Net offers the best accuracy-per-compute** — the core defense of the design decision.

**Standard practices, justified:**
- **Tile/mask pairing & normalization:** Standard in LULC pipelines; libraries like **Albumentations** apply matched transforms to image–mask tuples so masks stay aligned with augmented images (Towards Data Science tutorial). Pixel-wise classification of normalized tiles into LULC categories produces the map (ArcGIS/Esri workflow). Normalization stabilizes GPU training.
- **Data augmentation (rotation/flips):** For satellite imagery, *"it is common for practitioners to apply a limited set of transformations developed for natural images (e.g., flip and rotate) to expand the training set,"* and *"geometric transformations are beneficial for satellite imagery while color transformations generally are not"* (AAAI 2025, "Data Augmentation Approaches for Satellite Imagery"). Horizontal/vertical flips scored the highest accuracy for satellite classification (arXiv:2207.14580); square tiles allow 90° rotations and flips without changing dimensions (arXiv:1912.05067). Augmentation combats overfitting on limited labeled data and builds the rotation/reflection invariance appropriate to overhead (nadir) imagery, where there is no canonical "up."
- **Class imbalance / class weights:** *"Class imbalance is a common challenge in geospatial semantic segmentation — rare land cover types (e.g., wetlands, impervious surfaces) are often vastly outnumbered by dominant classes (e.g., forest, water). Standard cross-entropy loss treats all pixels equally, which can cause the model to underperform on minority classes"* (GeoAI/opengeoai.org). Standard remedies: **inverse-frequency class weights, weighted cross-entropy, focal loss** (Lin et al., 2017 — down-weights easy examples to focus on hard ones), **Dice loss** (region-based, robust to imbalance), Tversky, and **Unified Focal loss** (Yeung et al., 2021). Class weighting matters because LULC classes (built-up, wetland, bare) are highly imbalanced against dominant grassland/forest classes, which otherwise dominate the loss.
- **Cross-domain (India→Kenya) transfer — the key caveat:** Domain shift is well-recognized: *"the divergence of the multi-sensor images always challenge the transferability of deep learning,"* and *"severe distribution shifts of satellite imageries caused by geographical differences or acquisition conditions"* limit cross-region application (ScienceDirect; the Five-Billion-Pixels dataset paper). Established mitigations include **unsupervised domain adaptation** (LoveCS cross-sensor normalization; DACST two-stage adaptation; DANN adversarial approaches), **fine-tuning**, and **pseudo-labeling**. The report should flag that applying an India-trained model to Kenya risks reduced accuracy from spectral and land-cover distribution differences, and cite fine-tuning or domain adaptation on a small Kenyan labeled set as future work.

## Recommendations

1. **Frame the problem narrative in three escalating beats:** (a) open with the Maraga arrest (June 2026) and the Ritz-Carlton lawsuit as vivid hooks; (b) broaden to national statistics (68% wildlife decline, ~25% of Mau lost, 32.8% of global PAs under intense pressure); (c) pivot to the KWS ~KSh 12 billion funding gap to motivate cost-effective satellite monitoring as your solution.
2. **Defend U-Net explicitly** by citing Ronneberger et al. (2015, ≈88,770 citations), the Inria benchmark (U-Net best/near-best with low FLOPs), and the Western Ghats LISS-III Indian study (same data lineage as your training set). State plainly: under GPU/time constraints, U-Net delivers the best accuracy-per-compute.
3. **Pre-empt the domain-shift critique in your oral defense:** acknowledge India→Kenya distribution shift, cite the domain-adaptation literature (LoveCS, DACST, DANN), and propose fine-tuning on a small Kenyan labeled set as future work. This converts a weakness into evidence of technical maturity.
4. **Justify each design choice with a citation:** augmentation (AAAI 2025), class weighting (GeoAI; Lin et al. 2017; Yeung et al. 2021), and mask–image co-augmentation (Albumentations).
5. **Benchmarks/thresholds that would change the approach:** if validation mIoU on Kenyan tiles falls below ~0.5, or if minority-class IoU (e.g., built-up/bare ground) collapses toward zero, switch from class-weighted cross-entropy to **focal or Unified Focal loss** and add Kenyan **fine-tuning tiles**; if boundaries/edges are poor, evaluate **DeepLabv3+** as an alternative decoder.

## Caveats
- Several 2026-dated items (Maraga arrest, Ritz-Carlton dismissal) are recent news; details may still evolve, and 2026 legal outcomes should be re-checked before final submission.
- **Forest-cover figures vary by definition** (tree cover vs. gazetted forest vs. canopy-density threshold) and source (GFW/Hansen vs. KFS vs. FAO); each figure above is cited with its source so discrepancies are transparent rather than reconciled artificially.
- The **"306 wildebeest" latest-census figure** circulating online appears only on a non-authoritative site; this report uses the peer-reviewed figure (**~30,000 in the 1970s → ~509 by 2014**, Said et al. 2016) instead.
- **Applying an India-trained model to Kenya is scientifically valid for a capstone** but should be presented with explicit domain-shift caveats, not as production-grade accuracy. Quantitative accuracy claims should be backed by a Kenyan validation set, however small.
